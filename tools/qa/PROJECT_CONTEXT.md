HARD QUESTIONS
1. Why is this called drift if the datasets differ? We measured a cross-era, cross-source performance gap. A source classifier distinguished those sources with ROC AUC 0.9893. Collection method and template style can explain some of the gap. A clean test of time needs consistent collection over years.
2. Why did adding scams create so many false alarms? We hypothesize the model associated modern business wording and formatting with scam examples because legitimate training examples still came largely from older conversational SMS. Adding legitimate modern data reduced that mismatch. We did not prove which feature caused every error.
3. Why use logistic regression? It is a fast CPU baseline, easy to reproduce and explain, and lets us inspect features. The original research is an evaluation question, not a race to build the largest model.
4. What about split leakage? We removed normalized exact duplicates and checked near-template overlap. About 1.39% of modern test reports had a training near-copy at cosine similarity at least 0.90. A separate cluster-style sensitivity analysis exists. The original experiment did not prospectively reserve entire campaigns or sources, so a new campaign-held-out benchmark is still needed.
5. What do you miss? The original 2012 model caught 2 of 24 wrong-number openers and 0 of 4 parent impersonation examples. Four is too small for a population conclusion. A later high-stakes request may be absent from the pasted text.
6. Does it support other languages? We tested 8,866 non-English reports. The original 2012 model flagged about 50.8% in raw form and 69.6% on their dataset English translations. That is a diagnostic, not a shipping translation feature or accuracy guarantee.
7. Why care about false alarms? Repeated alerts can make people ignore real warnings. Measure both missed scams and false alerts on representative, consented legitimate messages and test whether users can decide what to do. A one-inbox test cannot tell us how often the average user will see alerts.
8. How do you explain a warning? The old linear model can show terms that influence its score. The live checker can show matching phrases for explicit requests. A contextual-only BERT flag cannot support an exact phrase-level claim. The demo’s RED example cites an actual verification-code request.
9. What data was used? UCI SMS Spam Collection, public IMC25 reports, a held-out-by-brand sample from an India inbox, genuine-style and scam-style synthetic controls, and a Portuguese Mozambique dataset used for a separate check. In the research outputs, redaction placeholders are preserved and numbers, contacts, and URLs are normalized. Credit the published datasets.
10. How was AI used to build it? The repo credits Claude Code for assistance with implementation and documentation, AI-written synthetic controls, and ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude as critics. Explain your own contributions and what you reviewed or ran. Say only what you and William actually did.
11. How would you improve it? Start with recent consented legitimate messages from many people and labeled scam reports under a consistent collection process. Hold out time, campaign and sender groups. Measure performance separately by scenario and language. Test warnings with older adults. Consider user-consented conversation context and independently verified sender signals only after designing the privacy boundaries.
12. Did you validate on older adults? No age or gender metadata and no measured older-adult usability outcome. Accessible copy, reading controls and actionable guidance are design decisions. The next step is a user test that measures comprehension and correct next action.
